Process or Artifact? Quantifying Genuine Social Influence Against Central Tendency in Multi-Agent LLM Deliberation

Process or Artifact? Quantifying Genuine Social Influence Against Central Tendency in Multi-Agent LLM Deliberation

Properties1
tagsresearch, deliberation, multi-agent, llm-agents, measurement, computational-social-science

2026년 7월 06일10 min read

A controlled decomposition of moderation in multi-agent LLM systems, with a synthetic-validation protocol and an intended extension to historical reconstruction. Research proposal — draft for discussion.

Abstract

When large language model agents deliberate on political questions, their positions tend to converge [1–4], and it is now well established that the model’s own biases — sycophancy, agreement bias, and a trained pull toward moderate, majority positions — contribute to this [5–8]. Recent work shows these artifacts are present, decomposes the drivers of agent opinion trajectories [1], and finds that convergence can persist even when sycophancy is suppressed [4]. What is less well studied is how much: comparatively little work directly quantifies what fraction of observed moderation is an artifact of the instrument, and what fraction, if any, is a genuine social process — agents actually updating in response to argument. The two are easily conflated, because an artifact that mimics deliberation is hard to detect precisely when it looks like the thing we expect [9].

We treat this as a controlled decomposition rather than a simulation of real politics. Each agent is assigned a scalar position and a supporting argument, which lets us fix the initial distribution and deliberately seed extremes, giving both experimental arms an identical starting point [10]. We then contrast a deliberation arm, in which agents exchange substantive cross-cutting arguments, against a content-stripped control matched on turn count, token budget, and interaction structure but with the deliberative content removed [11]. The gap between the two arms estimates the size of any genuine social process; equal contraction attributes the moderation to the instrument. We further separate apparent moderation into tail collapse — the loss of extreme positions, the model’s known failure mode [7, 8] — from genuine per-agent revision, so that a narrowing distribution is never mistaken for changed minds [12]. A synthetic pre-registration validates the method before any substantive run: we inject known no-change trajectories together with central tendency, and confirm that the control condition alone can manufacture apparent moderation.

The contribution is not a realistic simulation of politics but a diagnostic: a way to quantify, inside a multi-agent system deliberating on questions that have no right answer, how much of an observed moderation is a social process and how much is the instrument.

Why this matters, especially for AI for social science. A growing body of work uses LLM agents as stand-ins for human respondents and deliberators, and reads their aggregate behavior as evidence about opinion dynamics. That inference is only as trustworthy as our ability to tell modeled social behavior apart from the model’s own regularities. In the near term, this offers a concrete, reusable check for anyone running such simulations: a content-stripped control and a tail-collapse-versus-revision decomposition that flag when an apparent effect is an artifact before it is reported as a finding. In the longer term, a validated instrument of this kind is a precondition for the harder questions the field wants to ask — whether deliberation moderates or polarizes, whether these dynamics differ across eras or institutions, and eventually how simulated deliberation compares to real human deliberation (Phase 2 and beyond). The immediate contribution is methodological, but its purpose is to let downstream social-science claims rest on ground that has been checked rather than assumed.

Method design (Phase 1)

Both arms receive identical initialization; only the presence of deliberative content differs. Agents are initialized by directly assigning a scalar position (e.g., −3 to +3) plus a supporting argument, which lets us fully control the initial distribution and deliberately seed extremes. Initial distributions are designed, not drawn from real population or legislator proportions: this is a controlled discrimination, not a fidelity simulation, so realism at initialization would reintroduce the very artifact we mean to measure. A manipulation check re-reads each agent’s stance from its first utterance to confirm the assigned position was actually held.

Core decomposition (the paper’s heart). Apparent moderation (variance contraction) is split into a tail-collapse component (extremes disappear; minds unchanged, the distribution merely narrows) and a genuine-revision component (agents actually move). Applied within each arm: the control arm’s contraction is pure artifact; the deliberation arm’s excess contraction is a candidate social process; the tail-collapse-versus-revision ratio of that excess tells us whether it is persuasion or merely flattened extremes.

Decision logic.

Observed patternInterpretation
Both arms contract equallymoderation is entirely artifact
Deliberation excess, mostly tail collapseexcess is lost extremes, not persuasion (still artifact-side)
Deliberation excess, mostly individual revisiongenuine social-process component exists
Excess shrinks as issue identity-ladenness risesidentity issues resist persuasion; artifact dominates

Any pattern yields a publishable result — a quantitative “X% artifact, Y% process” answer — so there is no uninformative failure.

Phase 0 (synthetic pre-registration). Before any agent run, validate the measures against known ground truth: (i) inject no-change trajectories plus central tendency, and confirm the control-arm measures flag fabricated moderation; (ii) inject known tail collapse only, and confirm tail-mass change captures it while revision reads ≈ 0; (iii) inject known individual revision only, and confirm the reverse. This certifies the measurement pipeline before substantive runs, and is the first, scoop-free deliverable.

Scope. The full crossing is large (4 × 3 × 3 × 2); the MS runs one primary cell (bimodal × pure-extreme × symmetric × high-identity) to completion, with the rest as robustness. Precedents [13, 14] measure normative opinion change over time and recover per-agent latent state; the differentiator here is the combination of a deliberately extreme-seeded, fully controlled initial distribution with a content-stripped subtraction to isolate the process component. This combination, not the individual measures, is the contribution.

Appendix — intended extension (Phase 2: historical reconstruction)

Phase 1 builds and validates one tool: separating genuine change in an opinion distribution from the model’s tendency to collapse its tails toward the center. Phase 2 applies that tool to a more novel question — historical reconstruction. LLMs are increasingly used to reconstruct political discourse from earlier eras to ask whether politics has polarized over time [15–18]. This has a hidden dependency: if a model cannot faithfully reproduce the ideological spread of a past era — especially its extremes — then a finding that “the past was less polarized” may be an artifact of the instrument, not a fact about history [19, 20].

Phase 2 would test this with a three-way comparison of the ideological distribution for a given era: one anchored in period roll-call behavior [21], one from present-day human coders, and one reconstructed by the LLM. The triangulation separates genuine historical change from the era-distortion any present-day reader (human or model) imposes, and from the distortion specific to the LLM.

Ideological fidelity for present-day groups is an active area, and reconstruction of past opinion landscapes with time-locked models has begun — e.g., forecasting COVID-era polarization from a 2019-bounded model [22] — as has the study of “diversity collapse” in simulated populations [23] and regional polarization collapse in a Korean setting [24]. What appears comparatively less examined is the specific combination this program targets: whether, in historical reconstruction, tail collapse distorts the estimated longitudinal polarization trend itself, and quantifying that distortion against a behavioral anchor. History-LLM efforts focus largely on anachronism and style [19, 20]; tail-preservation in reconstructed ideological distributions, tied to a vote anchor, is what the Phase 1 tool is built to measure. Because reconstruction may be unreliable enough to undermine the baseline, Phase 2 depends on a small feasibility pilot before commitment; Phase 1 stands on its own regardless.
